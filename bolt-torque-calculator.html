How the calculation works

The classic torque-preload relation:

T = K × F × d
  • T — tightening torque (N·m)
  • K — torque coefficient (nut factor). 0.20 dry steel, 0.15 oiled, 0.12 with moly/anti-seize. Some standards use 0.16 as a neutral default.
  • F — target preload force (N) = preload% × proof strength Sp × tensile stress area As
  • d — nominal bolt diameter (m)

Tensile stress area comes from the ISO 898-1 relation As = 0.7854 × (d − 0.9743/n)² where n is threads per mm — the values in this calculator match the standard published tables (M8 → 36.6 mm², M10 → 58.0 mm², M12 → 84.3 mm²).

FAQ

Why is my joint still loose at the recommended torque?

Usually friction is higher than assumed (dry threads, rust, plating), the nut is turning on the flange rather than the threads, or the joint relaxed after first loading. Re-torque after the first thermal/mechanical cycle, or measure actual preload with a load-cell bolt or strain gauge.

Should I torque to yield?

No — for 90%+ preload you are close to the proof load; beyond ~95% you risk permanently stretching the bolt (turn-of-nut / yield-controlled tightening is a specialized procedure). For most reusable machinery joints, 65–75% of proof strength is the sweet spot.

How do grade 8.8 and SAE J429 Grade 5 compare?

Roughly equivalent: 8.8 (800 MPa tensile) ≈ Grade 5 (830 MPa). Metric 10.9 ≈ Grade 8 (1040 MPa). Don't mix imperial and metric markings on one joint — see the standards cross-reference.
